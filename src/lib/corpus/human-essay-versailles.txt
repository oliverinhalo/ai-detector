The Treaty of Versailles is usually taught as the cause of the Second World War, and I want to argue that this is only half true, and that the half which is false matters more.

The standard account runs like this. Germany was humiliated, saddled with reparations it could not pay, stripped of territory and forbidden an army. Resentment festered. Hitler promised to tear the treaty up and the German electorate, nursing its grievance, handed him the keys. Cause, effect, done.

The trouble is the timing. The treaty was signed in 1919. The Nazi vote in the 1928 Reichstag election was 2.6 per cent. If humiliation alone drove Germans to fascism, it took them nine years and an American banking collapse to notice. What changed between 1928 and 1932 was not the treaty, which had in fact been substantially softened by then, but unemployment: six million by the winter of 1932.

This does not mean Versailles was irrelevant. It gave the Nazis a story, and stories matter in politics more than economists like to admit. The "stab in the back" myth required a stab, and the treaty supplied one. But a story needs an audience in the mood to hear it, and that mood came from the Depression.

What bothers me about the standard account is what it implies about the present. If harsh peace terms produce war, the lesson is to be gentle with defeated enemies. If mass unemployment produces extremism, the lesson is something quite different and considerably more uncomfortable, because it is about us and not about them.

Keynes saw some of this in 1919, though not the part he is usually credited with. He thought the reparations unpayable, and he was right about the arithmetic. He also thought a prosperous Germany was the condition of a peaceful Europe, which turned out to be the more important point and the one nobody acted on until 1948.
