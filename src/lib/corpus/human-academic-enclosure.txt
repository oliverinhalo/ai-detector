The historiography of the enclosure movement has shifted markedly since Thompson, and not entirely for the better. Where an earlier generation of scholars treated parliamentary enclosure as a straightforward expropriation of the commons — a legal theft dressed in the language of improvement — more recent quantitative work has emphasised regional variation, the modest size of many enclosure awards, and the survival of customary rights well into the nineteenth century.

Allen's estimates, drawn from the Midland counties, suggest yield increases of perhaps eleven per cent attributable to enclosure itself, considerably below the figures assumed by contemporaries. Turner reached broadly similar conclusions using a different sample. If enclosure was the engine of agricultural revolution, it was a surprisingly inefficient one.

Yet the revisionist case has been pressed too far. The aggregate yield question, however carefully answered, is not the question that mattered to those who lost access to fuel, pasture, and gleaning rights. Humphries demonstrated three decades ago that common rights constituted a substantial share of household income for cottagers, and that their loss fell disproportionately on women and children. A model that counts bushels per acre and ignores the household economy will necessarily understate the social cost.

There is a further methodological difficulty. Enclosure awards survive unevenly; the parishes that generated the fullest documentation were not a random sample of English parishes. Counties with heavy common-field acreage are over-represented, and the pastoral west is largely absent from the standard datasets. Inferences about national effects rest on a base that is both incomplete and systematically skewed.

What is required, I would suggest, is not a further round of aggregate estimation but a return to the parish. The records of the Court of Quarter Sessions, underused by economic historians, contain depositions in which cottagers describe in their own words what access to the waste was worth to them. That evidence will not yield an elasticity. It will, however, tell us something the elasticities cannot.
