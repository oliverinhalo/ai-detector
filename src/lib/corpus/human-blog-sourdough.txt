Here's the thing about sourdough: nobody tells you how boring it is.

I started a starter in April 2020, like everyone else, and I want to be honest about what the first six weeks were actually like. You feed a jar of flour paste twice a day. It smells like nail varnish for a while. Then it smells like beer. Then, if you're lucky, it smells like yoghurt, and that's when you can use it, except mine went back to nail varnish for a fortnight in June and I nearly threw it out.

Let's talk about hydration, because this is where every guide loses people.

Hydration is just the ratio of water to flour by weight. Seventy per cent hydration means 700g water to 1000g flour. The guides all push you toward 80 or 85 because high-hydration loaves have those big irregular holes that photograph well. What they don't mention is that 85% dough handles like wet cement and you will spend your first ten bakes scraping it off the counter with a dough scraper and swearing.

Start at 65. Honestly. Sixty-five per cent is a dough you can actually pick up.

My oven is a cheap electric fan oven that runs about 15 degrees cold, which I only discovered after buying a £6 oven thermometer, which is the single best purchase I've made in this entire hobby. Every recipe failure I'd blamed on my starter, my flour, my technique, my water — it was the oven. Fifteen degrees.

So what actually matters? Three things, as far as I can tell after four years and something like three hundred loaves. Temperature of the dough during bulk ferment, which you control with where you put the bowl. Shaping tension, which you learn with your hands and cannot learn from a video. And an oven you've actually measured.

Everything else is noise. The banneton, the scoring patterns, the specific brand of flour, the Dutch oven versus the baking steel. I own all of it. None of it moved the needle like the thermometer did.

I'm not saying don't enjoy the gear. I'm saying the gear is a hobby inside the hobby, and it's fine to notice that's what's happening.
